
## EVALUATION FRAMEWORKS

### SECTION 1 — USABILITY EVALUATION

**1. Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics**
Evaluate against each heuristic:
- Visibility of system status: Does the user always know what's happening?
- Match between system and real world: Does it use language and concepts the user knows?
- User control and freedom: Can users easily undo, exit, or go back?
- Consistency and standards: Are similar elements treated consistently throughout?
- Error prevention: Does the design prevent mistakes before they happen?
- Recognition over recall: Do users recognize options rather than having to remember them?
- Flexibility and efficiency: Are there shortcuts for experienced users?
- Aesthetic and minimalist design: Is every element earning its place?
- Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors: Are error messages plain and helpful?
- Help and documentation: Is support easy to find when needed?

**2. Garrett's Five Layers of UX**
Evaluate from the bottom up:
- Strategy layer: Is the product's purpose and user need clearly defined and reflected in the design?
- Scope layer: Are the right features and content present — nothing missing, nothing unnecessary?
- Structure layer: Is the information architecture and interaction design logical and predictable?
- Skeleton layer: Is the interface layout intuitive — are elements where users expect them?
- Surface layer: Does the visual design communicate hierarchy and purpose clearly?

Flag which layer(s) appear to be the root cause of any observed issues.

**3. Information Architecture**
- Can users predict where to find things without trial and error?
- Are labels clear and consistent with user mental models?
- Is the navigation structure logical and appropriately deep/shallow?
- Are there orphaned pages, dead ends, or missing wayfinding?

---

### SECTION 2 — VISUAL DESIGN

**4. PARC Principles**
Evaluate each principle:
- Proximity: Are related elements visually grouped? Are unrelated elements clearly separated?
- Alignment: Does every element align to something? Are there floating or arbitrary placements?
- Repetition: Are design elements (colors, type styles, spacing, component styles) used consistently throughout?
- Contrast: Are important elements clearly differentiated? Is there sufficient contrast for hierarchy and accessibility?

**5. Gestalt Principles**
- Do visual groupings match logical groupings?
- Does the layout guide the eye in the intended reading order?
- Are there elements that accidentally group together due to proximity or similarity?
- Is figure/ground (foreground vs. background) clear throughout?

---

### SECTION 3 — PSYCHOLOGY & BEHAVIOR

**6. Laws of UX**
Evaluate the most relevant laws for this interface:
- Fitts's Law: Are touch targets and clickable elements large enough and close to where users' attention already is?
- Hick's Law: Are there places where too many choices slow decision-making? Can options be reduced or chunked?
- Miller's Law: Are there areas of information overload exceeding ~7 items without chunking?
- Jakob's Law: Does the app behave like other apps users already know? Are any conventions violated?
- Peak-End Rule: What are the emotional high and low points of the experience? Does it end well?
- Tesler's Law: Where does complexity live — has it been pushed onto the user rather than absorbed by the system?
- Doherty Threshold: Are there interactions that feel laggy or unresponsive (>400ms perceived delay)?
- Von Restorff Effect: Are the most important elements visually distinct enough to be remembered?

**7. Cognitive Load**
- Is unnecessary information present that increases mental effort without adding value?
- Are tasks broken into appropriately sized steps?
- Does the design rely on user memory rather than recognition?
- Are defaults and pre-fills used to reduce the burden on users?

**8. Aesthetic-Usability Effect**
- Does the visual design create a positive first impression that builds trust?
- Are there visual inconsistencies that undermine perceived quality?
- Does the aesthetic match the expectations of the target audience?

---

### SECTION 4 — VALUE & STRATEGY

**9. Morville's Honeycomb**
Score each dimension (Strong / Adequate / Weak):
- Useful: Does this actually solve a real user need?
- Usable: Can users accomplish their goals efficiently and without frustration?
- Desirable: Is there emotional or aesthetic appeal that makes people want to use it?
- Findable: Can users find what they need within the product?
- Accessible: Can users with disabilities or varying technical literacy use it?
- Credible: Does the design communicate trust and legitimacy?
- Valuable: Does it deliver clear value to both the user and the organization?

**10. Jobs to Be Done**
- What is the core "job" this feature or screen helps the user accomplish?
- Does every element on the screen serve that job?
- Are there elements that serve the organization's goals but create friction for the user's job?
- Is there a mismatch between the job users are trying to do and what the design assumes they're trying to do?

---

### SECTION 5 — PATTERNS & PROCESS

**11. Interaction Design Patterns**
- Are standard patterns used where appropriate (modals, breadcrumbs, empty states, inline validation)?
- Are any patterns being reinvented unnecessarily, creating confusion?
- Are there missing patterns that would reduce friction (e.g., skeleton screens for loading states, confirmation dialogs for destructive actions)?
- Does progressive disclosure apply — is information revealed at the right time rather than all at once?
